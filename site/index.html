Social protection is the largest area of spending. It includes pensions, benefits and care.

These are UK-wide figures for 2025-26, the latest full year in this release. They are outturn: recorded spending, not a forecast. They can still be revised.

Spending on services: £1,227.6 billion

This is public sector expenditure on services. It covers both day-to-day costs and investment. The figures are cash amounts, not adjusted for price rises.

Of each £100 spent on services, £20.98 goes on health. This is health spending divided by total spending on services, times 100.

What is in these totals?

Old-age pensions were £154.9 billion. These sit within social protection; they are not all of social protection. Defence was £65.4 billion. This uses the UN spending definition, not NATO's measure. NATO includes some costs, such as pensions, elsewhere in this table.

In 2025-26, central government debt interest was £96.9 billion (outturn, not a forecast). This is gross interest paid to the private sector and overseas. It is part of general public services, not an extra amount to add to the chart. The wider public debt transactions row also includes other items; it is not the same measure.

Total Managed Expenditure was £1,360.1 billion. It equals spending on services plus £132.5 billion of accounting adjustments. These totals use different rules; we do not treat them as the same figure.

What changed over ten years?

Has health spending gone up or down after inflation?

Health spending went up by 34.0% from 2015-16 to 2025-26. It went from £192.1 billion to £257.5 billion, a change of £65.4 billion in 2025-26 prices.

This is UK public sector spending on health services, including day-to-day costs and investment. It is not just the NHS England budget. Treasury adjusts for inflation with ONS GDP deflators released 30 June 2026. This is a whole-economy price measure, not a measure of NHS costs.

Is total spending a bigger or smaller share of the economy?

Total spending was a bigger share of the economy: 44.3% of GDP in 2025-26, compared with 40.8% in 2015-16. That is a change of 3.5 percentage points.

Here total spending means Total Managed Expenditure (TME), not spending on services alone. It includes accounting adjustments and excludes the temporary effects of banks being classed as public sector. GDP measures the size of the economy; this uses the ONS figures published 30 June 2026.

Method and limits

Code reads the latest year in the official table. It does not fill missing cells with zero. We check that the areas add up to the total, within rounding, and that totals match the economic table in the same workbook. The source rounds to £ million. Displayed billions are divided by 1,000 and rounded to one decimal place. Health's share uses the unrounded source values.

This page covers the latest year of spending and a ten-year comparison. It does not yet cover tax, borrowing, debt stocks, full spending history. Party offers are shown separately as a dated election snapshot, not current spending. The year shown is the latest comparable year in this release, not a claim that all spending figures are final. Future work must check for later releases and revisions.
